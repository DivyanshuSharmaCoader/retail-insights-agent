# Kestrel Retail Promotions Playbook (2026)

*Kestrel Retail is a fictional company. This document is synthetic and exists to demonstrate retrieval.*

## Purpose

This playbook sets the rules every category team follows when planning, approving and reviewing a promotion. A promotion is any temporary price reduction that applies to a whole category or a named set of products.

## Approval rules

- Every promotion needs a written business case approved by the Category Head and the Finance Business Partner at least 21 days before the start date.
- The maximum discount on any promotion is **30%** of list price. Anything deeper needs approval from the Chief Commercial Officer.
- Promotions on the same category must be at least 30 days apart, so that the baseline period is clean.
- Promotions cannot be stacked. A product can be in only one active promotion at a time, and loyalty coupons do not apply on top of a category discount.

## Success target

A promotion is considered successful when **net revenue per day during the promotion is at least 20% higher than the average net revenue per day in the 4 weeks before it starts** (the baseline period). Net revenue means revenue after the discount.

Campaigns that miss the 20% uplift target are flagged in the monthly commercial review, and the category team must explain whether the shortfall came from stock availability, weak communication or a discount that was too shallow.

A secondary check is gross margin: the promotion should not reduce the category's gross margin for the month by more than 3 percentage points.

## Post-promotion review

- The category team submits a post-promotion review within **14 days** of the end date.
- The review must include: uplift versus baseline, units sold, discount cost, stock-outs during the campaign and customer feedback.
- Results are added to the promotions calendar so that future campaigns can be compared like for like.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing promotion revenue with the same period last year instead of the 4-week baseline. Seasonality makes this misleading, especially for beverages in summer.
- Running a promotion while the main supplier has a fill-rate problem. The extra demand then turns into stock-outs and complaints.
- Using a shallow discount (10% or less) on low-frequency categories such as household products. Historically these rarely reach the uplift target.
